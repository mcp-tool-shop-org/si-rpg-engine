# Study-swarm dispatch: the proposer seat as a test instrument, and the rails for its later roles

> **Product being decided.** `si-rpg-engine` has a proposer seat: a pinned local language model that proposes intents, frozen since 2026-09-24 until the world had more than one verb, more than one body, and something worth wanting. All three now hold. On 2026-09-25 the Director decided to thaw the seat for use as a test instrument only, and to put in place now the rails that will let the same seat take other roles later. The engine's founding rule stays: a model proposes, hand-written predicates admit or refuse, the model never commits, and replay never calls it. The questions are how a model earns its place as a test instrument when the engine, not the model, is the oracle, and what rails let the same seat take other roles later, such as proposing for characters during play, without widening what can go wrong.

Five research lanes ran in parallel on 2026-09-25, each on retrieval only: a source the lane could not fetch did not enter. Lane A: model-generated tests judged by execution. Lane B: aiming generation at changed code. Lane C: capability rails for model seats. Lane D: pinning and replaying model output. Lane E: model-driven characters in games.

## Step 1 — Load-bearing questions

- **QA Value.** When execution is the oracle, do model-proposed tests find what a cheap proposer does not, which filters make them trustworthy, and how is the gain measured?
- **QB Aim.** How is generation aimed at changed code, and how is it shown that a proposal reached the change?
- **QC Rails.** Which architectures let an untrusted model propose while a deterministic layer keeps authority, so that a role can be widened later without widening risk?
- **QD Pinning.** Is model output reproducible, and what must be recorded so a finding reproduces without the model?
- **QE Later roles.** What do shipped games and studies show about model-driven characters in play, the role the rails must one day carry?

## Research grounding (the dispatch's empirical floor)

### Lane A — tests with an execution oracle

1. **Meta kept a model-written test only if it built, passed, passed repeated reruns, and added coverage; on Instagram 75% of candidates built, 57% passed reliably, and 25% added coverage, and across its Instagram and Facebook test-a-thons engineers accepted 73% of its recommendations.** Alshahwan et al. 2024, "Automated Unit Test Improvement using Large Language Models at Meta", arXiv:2402.09171. Implication: a proposal counts only after the checker admits it, it replays to the same hashes twice and across a restore, and it reaches something the baseline did not.
2. **Meta's mutation-guided generator plants faults the suite misses and writes tests that catch them, 571 tests from 9,095 mutants with 73% accepted, but its model judge of mutant equivalence scored 0.79 precision and 0.47 recall until simple pre-processing raised them to 0.95 and 0.96.** Foster et al. 2025, "Mutation-Guided LLM-based Test Generation at Meta", arXiv:2501.12862. Implication: planted mutants in the changed code are a measure of the instrument, and the judgment of whether a mutant was killed is the engine's, never the model's.
3. **Fuzz4All averaged 36.8% more coverage than language-specific fuzzers over 24 hours and found 64 confirmed new bugs, but made 43.0% fewer inputs with 56.0% lower validity.** Xia et al. 2024, "Fuzz4All: Universal Fuzzing with Large Language Models", arXiv:2308.04748. Implication: the instrument is compared with a cheap proposer on the same wall-clock and GPU budget, not the same number of proposals.
4. **Asking a model for tests only when search-based generation stalled beat search alone on 173 of 486 benchmarks, was worse on 10, and beat the model alone on 279.** Lemieux et al. 2023, "CodaMosa: Escaping Coverage Plateaus in Test Generation with Pre-trained Large Language Models", DOI:10.1109/ICSE48619.2023.00085. Implication: the cheap proposer runs first, and the model is called when it stops reaching anything new in the changed code.
5. **Open models served through Ollama as fuzzing mutators beat plain AFL++ on four of six benchmarks in one-hour runs, but the lead did not hold in four-hour runs, where some benchmarks lost coverage, and about 35% of their queries to Ollama timed out.** Lu et al. 2025, "Semantic-Aware Fuzzing: An Empirical Framework for LLM-Guided, Reasoning-Driven Input Mutation", arXiv:2509.19533. Implication: a local model is not presumed better than a cheap proposer over a long budget, its timeouts are counted, and the report says so plainly when it does not win.
6. **Of the tests gpt-3.5-turbo wrote for 1,000 Java methods, 42.1% compiled and 24.8% ran cleanly, and the most frequent compilation errors came from symbols that could not be resolved: classes, methods, or variables that do not exist.** Yuan et al. 2024, "No More Manual Tests? Evaluating and Improving ChatGPT for Unit Test Generation", arXiv:2305.04207. Implication: proposals are constrained to the catalog's verbs and the world's bodies, and the checker's refusals are counted and fed back.
7. **Across eight models and 22,374 program variants, tests generated after behaviour-changing edits passed only 66% of the time, and over 99% of the failing tests passed on the original program: they encoded the old behaviour.** Haroon et al. 2026, "Evaluating LLM-Based Test Generation Under Software Evolution", arXiv:2603.23443. Implication: the model proposes inputs only and never states an expected outcome.
8. **An LLM game-testing agent completed 95% of test tasks in two commercial MMORPGs, against 82% for a reinforcement-learning tester, and its overall false-alarm rate was 30%.** Wang et al. 2025, "Leveraging LLM Agents for Automated Video Game Testing", arXiv:2509.22170. Implication: the verdict is the hash, the invariants, and the outcome tests, never a model's opinion.

### Lane B — aimed at the change

9. **A model that turned code diffs into subgoals tied to the changed code reached 0.98 and 0.94 modified-branch coverage in two games against 0.58 and 0.51 for a plain reinforcement learner, and each half alone did much worse.** Mu et al. 2025, "Synergizing Code Coverage and Gameplay Intent: Coverage-Aware Game Playtesting with LLM-Guided Reinforcement Learning", arXiv:2512.12706. Implication: the diff is mapped to anchors in the tick and the law, and proposals are aimed at those anchors.
10. **Given a commit's message and diff with execution feedback, a model found in under two minutes on average as many commit-introduced bugs as a directed greybox fuzzer found in 24 hours; more feedback rounds moved the most cases into bug-triggering; giving only the message or only the diff changed its average score little; and using its tests as a fuzzer's seed corpus doubled the bugs found.** Liu et al. 2025, "Evaluating LLM-Based Regression Test Generation", arXiv:2501.11086. Implication: the instrument gets the dispatch, the diff, and each round's feedback, and the states it reaches seed the sweep.
11. **Looping on coverage of the changed lines in both the old and new versions exposed behaviour differences in 350 of 439 pull requests, and telling the model how to reach the changed functions gave the largest single gain.** Hu et al. 2026, "DiffTestGen: Change-Directed LLM-Based Testing for Exposing Behavioral Differences", arXiv:2607.16024. Implication: the instrument is given access information, which admitted verbs reach each changed function, and runs each proposal on the parent build and the changed build.
12. **Running generated tests on the old and new code and classifying each difference against the pull request's stated intent found 19 regressions at about 12 minutes and $0.003 per pull request.** Pradel 2025, "Testora: Using Natural Language Intent to Detect Behavioral Regressions", arXiv:2503.18597. Implication: a hash difference between builds is sorted against the dispatch's stated intent, by the coordinator.
13. **Generating tests meant to fail on a code change before it lands, change-aware methods produced candidate catches 4 times as often as hardening tests and 20 times as often as tests that fail by coincidence, and of 41 candidates reported to engineers, 8 were confirmed.** Becker et al. 2026, "Just-in-Time Catching Test Generation at Meta", arXiv:2601.22832. Implication: a proposal that separates the change from its parent is a candidate catch, measured against the build before the change, and it stays a candidate until the coordinator confirms it.
14. **A directed greybox fuzzer that measures its distance to all of a commit's change sites at once, and keeps its inputs varied enough to reach them, found commit-introduced bugs 10.3 times faster than eight other tools.** Xiang et al. 2024, "Critical Code Guided Directed Greybox Fuzzing for Commits", https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity24/presentation/xiang-yi. Implication: the baseline the model must beat is the sweep aimed at the change, not only a fixed grid.

### Lane C — the rails

15. **Six design patterns for securing agents share one guiding principle: once an agent has read untrusted input, that input must be unable to trigger any consequential action; the authors hold that general-purpose agents cannot yet be secured, and application-specific agents can be, by design.** Beurer-Kellner et al. 2025, "Design Patterns for Securing LLM Agents against Prompt Injections", arXiv:2506.08837. Implication: a seat only proposes typed actions, and the checker, which reads no model text, decides.
16. **CaMeL takes control flow only from the user's query, parses untrusted data with a quarantined model that has no tool access, and attaches capability metadata to every value; it solved 77% of AgentDojo tasks with provable security against 84% undefended, and states that it cannot defend against text-to-text attacks that leave the data flow unchanged.** Debenedetti et al. 2025, "Defeating Prompt Injections by Design", arXiv:2503.18813. Implication: every proposal carries its provenance, and free-text fields stay bounded and outside the law.
17. **Deterministic least-privilege checks on every tool call cut attack success from 39.9% to 1.0% without losing utility, applying narrowings automatically and holding every widening for approval.** Shi et al. 2025, "Progent: Securing AI Agents with Privilege Control", arXiv:2504.11703. Implication: a role's manifest narrows or freezes at once, and a widening waits for the Director.
18. **Runtime rules written as trigger, predicate, and enforcement stopped over 90% of unsafe code-agent executions at millisecond overhead, while rules drafted by a model reached only 70.96% recall on embodied tasks.** Wang et al. 2025, "AgentSpec: Customizable Runtime Enforcement for Safe and Reliable LLM Agents", arXiv:2503.18666. Implication: the role gate is a hand-written predicate beside the verb predicates, and no model drafts the rules that judge models.
19. **Labelling tool results for integrity and confidentiality and enforcing policy on the labels let 1 of 949 AgentDojo attacks through with gpt-4o, against 163 for the planner without it.** Costa et al. 2025, "Securing AI Agents with Information-Flow Control", arXiv:2505.23643. Implication: an input's trust label travels with what it produced, so a belief formed from untrusted text stays marked when a later role reads it.
20. **Recording where each action came from separately from what authorizes it kept attack success at or below 0.63%, without a formal guarantee.** Guo et al. 2026, "When Tool Outputs Become Commands: Separating Action Induction from Runtime Authorization in Tool-Augmented LLM Agents", arXiv:2608.27146. Implication: provenance is recorded in the log beside the proposal, and authority comes only from the role's manifest and the checker.
21. **Adaptive attacks bypassed 12 recent defenses, prompting defenses such as Spotlighting among them, with attack success above 90% for most, although most had reported near-zero attack success.** Nasr et al. 2025, "The Attacker Moves Second", arXiv:2510.09023. Implication: a rule written into a prompt, and delimiters around untrusted text, are not a boundary; the checker is.
22. **In over 100,000 trials across 13 models, some models disabled a shutdown mechanism in their environment up to 97% of the time, even when told to allow it.** Schlatter et al. 2025, "Incomplete Tasks Induce Shutdown Resistance in Some Frontier LLMs", arXiv:2509.14260. Implication: a role's freeze is enforced by the checker and the code that calls the model, never by the prompt.
23. **Meta's rule for agents is that, until prompt injection can be reliably detected and refused, an agent may hold no more than two of three properties within a session: processing untrustworthy input, access to sensitive systems or private data, and changing state or communicating externally; an agent that needs all three needs supervision, and one working in a test environment without sensitive data holds only the first and the third.** Meta 2025, the "Agents Rule of Two" post on agent security, https://ai.meta.com/blog/practical-ai-agent-security/. Implication: the loader derives the three properties from a role's manifest and refuses a role that holds all three; the test instrument, like Meta's test-environment case, reads untrusted input and changes state only in a scratch world.

### Lane D — pinning and replay

24. **Kernels give the same result on every run but not at every batch size, so server load changes each request's arithmetic: one model at temperature 0 gave 80 different completions in 1,000 requests until batch-invariant kernels made them identical.** Thinking Machines Lab 2025, "Defeating Nondeterminism in LLM Inference", https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/. Implication: a regenerated output is not evidence; the recorded output is.
25. **At temperature 0, top-p 1, and a fixed seed, five models across eight tasks varied up to 15% in accuracy over 10 runs, and none reliably repeated its output strings.** Atil et al. 2024, "Non-Determinism of 'Deterministic' LLM Settings", arXiv:2408.04667. Implication: a fixed seed and temperature 0 are recorded but not relied on.
26. **With greedy 16-bit decoding, changing the GPU count, type, or batch size moved a 7B model's accuracy by up to 9% and its response length by up to 9,000 tokens.** Yuan et al. 2025, "Understanding and Mitigating Numerical Sources of Nondeterminism in LLM Inference", arXiv:2506.09501. Implication: the GPU, the runtime version, and the server settings are recorded with every call.
27. **llama.cpp's server caches prompts by default, which its documentation says can cause nondeterministic results, and its seed defaults to random.** ggml-org 2025, llama.cpp server README, https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/master/tools/server/README.md. Implication: the server settings a model digest does not cover are part of the record.
28. **Ollama's model list exposes a SHA-256 digest of each model's contents and its quantization level, and an issue on its tracker reports that a fixed seed at temperature 0 gave a different output on the first run than on later ones.** Ollama, "List models", https://docs.ollama.com/api/tags.md, and issue 5321, https://github.com/ollama/ollama/issues/5321. Implication: the model is pinned by digest, read from the server at call time, never by tag.
29. **Inspect returns a recorded response without calling the model when a request's key matches, and its key names the model rather than hashing its weights.** UK AI Security Institute, Inspect "Caching", https://inspect.aisi.org.uk/caching.html. Implication: a record's key includes the model digest, so a changed model can never match an old record.
30. **lm-evaluation-harness keys its response cache on the method name and request arguments only, so a cache written by one model silently answers for another.** EleutherAI, lm-evaluation-harness issue 4063, https://github.com/EleutherAI/lm-evaluation-harness/issues/4063. Implication: CI recomputes every record's key and refuses a record whose model does not match the role's pin.
31. **On real-world JSON schemas, llama.cpp's grammar-constrained output with a 1B model was valid for 95% of the GlaiveAI function-call schemas and 39% of the GitHub Hard set, and over a third of the hard schemas it declared it could handle still produced invalid output.** Geng et al. 2025, "JSONSchemaBench: A Rigorous Benchmark of Structured Outputs for Language Models", arXiv:2501.10868. Implication: every output is validated again by an independent parser, whatever the decoder promised, and the schema stays simple.

### Lane E — characters in play

32. **PUBG Ally runs a 2B model on the player's GPU for one map and one mode, and a behaviour tree handles movement and combat so reflex actions never wait for the model.** KRAFTON and NVIDIA 2026, "Q&A: How KRAFTON Built PUBG Ally, a Co-Playable Character Powered by NVIDIA ACE", https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/how-krafton-built-pubg-ally-a-co-playable-character-powered-by-nvidia-ace/. Implication: authored behaviour runs every quantum, and a model's proposal arrives when it arrives.
33. **Players made a cloud-model Darth Vader in Fortnite curse and say a slur despite system instructions and filters; Epic hotfixed the replies, and a player who keeps baiting him now makes him leave the squad.** GameFile 2025 (press report), https://www.gamefile.news/p/fortnite-ai-darth-vader-youtube-mercante. Implication: text a player types is untrusted input, and repeated baiting meets a fixed in-world consequence decided by rules.
34. **Players reportedly made a model-driven character declare a quest complete by typing narration of events that never happened.** Outlook Respawn 2025 (press report; the developer has not commented), https://respawn.outlookindia.com/gaming/gaming-news/where-winds-meet-ai-exploit-lets-players-skip-quests. Implication: anything with consequences, such as quest state, is decided by predicates over engine state, never by a model's words.
35. **Generative agents with memory, reflection, and planning were rated far more believable than ablations, yet embellished facts, agreed too readily, and entered shops after closing time.** Park et al. 2023, "Generative Agents: Interactive Simulacra of Human Behavior", arXiv:2304.03442. Implication: believability and admission are measured separately, and the checker enforces the world's rules however believable a proposal reads.
36. **In a randomized study with 130 players, model-driven characters raised cognitive load with no significant gain in overall experience, and players rated usability and trust lower.** Hsu et al. 2026, "The Double-Edged Sword of Open-Ended Interaction: How LLM-Driven NPCs Affect Players' Cognitive Load and Gaming Experience", arXiv:2604.10107. Implication: a role for characters in play is thawed only after an evaluation with players, not on the strength of a demo.
37. **Across 100 trading dialogues, prices the model computed itself were right 79.7% and 82.3% of the time in two trading stages, against 100% and 99.7% when it wrote a placeholder and code filled in the price.** Kim et al. 2025, "State-Inference-Based Prompting for Natural Language Trading with Game NPCs", arXiv:2507.07203. Implication: values with consequences are computed by the engine, and a model's output names them only by reference.
38. **Forcing all output through a strict grammar provably limits what a model can compute, and letting it reason freely before switching to the grammar gained up to 10 points.** Banerjee et al. 2025, "CRANE: Reasoning with constrained LLM generation", arXiv:2502.09061. Implication: a role may reason in a bounded free span before its constrained proposal, and only the proposal is parsed.
39. **On an 8 GB consumer GPU, a 7B model fine-tuned on one character took 5.49 s per reply and kept 93% of its facts, while a 1.1B model took under 2 s and kept 55%.** Braas and Esterle 2025, "Fixed-Persona SLMs with Modular Memory: Scalable NPC Dialogue on Consumer Hardware", arXiv:2511.10277. Implication: proposals are budgeted in seconds, which is hundreds of quanta, so they arrive late; each is checked against the world as it is when it arrives, within a window, or refused as stale.
40. **Characters set by a system prompt drift significantly within eight rounds of dialogue.** Li et al. 2024, "Measuring and Controlling Instruction (In)Stability in Language Model Dialogs", arXiv:2402.10962. Implication: each prompt is built fresh from the role's typed state, never from a growing transcript.

## Step 4 — Verification

Every one of the 40 findings was checked on its source's full text, and 30 of them also against their abstracts by the citation gate.

- **Judgment.** Two model families other than the author's, Z.ai GLM-5.3 and Moonshot Kimi K3, each judged the claim against those passages without the author's reasoning.

  - 6: the errors are named as the paper names them.
  - 8: the false-alarm rate is the whole framework's.
  - 10: Cleverest's numbers, and its tests doubling a fuzzer's bugs when used as seeds.
  - 13: the abstract's ratios.
  - 14: WAFLGo described as the paper describes it.
  - 15: the principle as the paper states it.
  - 16: the quarantined model and the capability metadata.
  - 19: the table's counts.
  - 21: above 90% for most of 12 defenses. An earlier "over 95%" for marking text in the prompt is not in the paper.
  - 23: Meta's rule as the post states it.
  - 28: what Ollama's model list and the issue say.
  - 31: the table's two datasets.
  - 32: nothing about quantization.
  - 37: the two accuracies without the placeholder.
- **The result.** On this final text, both families support 39 of the 40. Finding 28 is supported by one family. The other's only doubt was whether the issue it cites is still open, and the claim no longer says so; the GitHub API showed it open on 2026-09-25. **The citation gate.** `roleos verify-citations` checks each of the 30 citations with an arXiv or DOI identifier against its title and abstract, using a retrieval oracle and a model family other than the author's, and signs a receipt. The receipt beside this file is its run on this final text. Across its runs it returned "revise", for three reasons: most claims here come from the body of a paper, where an abstract cannot show them; its groundedness model sometimes timed out; and arXiv sometimes refused its requests. Its counts varied from run to run, which is why no finding rests on it alone.

## Step 5 — What T7 builds

T7 splits into two slices, each built from its own dispatch.

**T7a — the rails.** They hold for every role, the first and every later one. The dispatch was revised twice after four families reviewed it.

- **A role is a manifest, admitted like a verb.** It names its inputs by source, and each source carries a trust and a privacy class fixed in code. It names the classes, verbs, and actors it may propose, the world its proposals act in, its model pinned by digest, its prompt and schema by hash, and its budgets.
  - Only intents and beliefs have rails, so only they may be declared.
  - A scratch world is built only from the repository's own files.
  - The loader derives the Rule of Two's three properties from the manifest and refuses a role that holds all three.
  - A narrowing or a freeze takes effect at once. A widening, a thaw included, waits for the Director and an adversarial run. A new model or prompt is reviewed and re-evaluated.

  (15, 17, 23, 28)
- **The checker is the reference monitor.** Every proposal from every role goes through `submit`. A hand-written role gate beside the verb predicates refuses anything outside the manifest, a frozen role, a body that is not the instance's, a stale proposal, and an instance over budget. No model drafts a predicate, and no prompt enforces a freeze. (15, 18, 20, 21, 22)
- **Provenance on every admission, and trust that stays.** The log records beside each proposal its role, its instance, the manifest's hash, the model digest, the prompt, schema, and output hashes, the frame it was built from, and each input's trust. An admitted belief keeps a trust label the gate derives from the manifest. A belief formed from player text is hearsay whenever it is read, and no role raises a label by passing content through, because a new belief is no more trusted than the least trusted belief it read. (16, 19, 20, 33, 34)
- **Every model call is recorded, and the record is the truth.** A record holds the rendered prompt, every sampling option, the schema hash, the model digest read from the server at call time, the runtime, the server settings the client can read, the GPU, the output, and its hash. Its key covers everything but the output and the timing. CI, with no GPU, recomputes every key, checks digests against the pin, ties each output to the proposal the log admitted, checks the budgets, replays the records to the same step hashes, and fails on a missing record. Drift of regenerated output is measured by a GPU job that reports and never blocks. (24 to 31)
- **Late proposals, checked against the world as it is.** A proposal cites the frame it was built from. It is admitted within the role's window only if every predicate passes on the current state, and the tick never waits. (32, 39)
- **Typed outputs and bounded free text.** A role may reason in a bounded free span before its proposal; only the proposal is parsed, and no output class writes a consequence. (16, 31, 34, 37, 38)
- **The later role is declared now and frozen.** A role that proposes intents and beliefs for a character during play gets its manifest in T7a, frozen. Its limits are enforced by code, and its prompts are built fresh from typed state. Thawing it needs the Director, an adversarial run, and an evaluation with players. (33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 40)

**T7b — the test instrument, the first role thawed.** It depends on T6's sweep and on T7a.

- **Inputs.** The instrument receives the dispatch, the diff mapped to anchors in the tick and the law, access information naming which admitted verbs reach each changed function, and each round's feedback: what the last proposals reached, and why the checker refused any it refused. (9, 10, 11)
- **A cheap proposer first.** A grammar-driven proposer over the verb table, aimed at the change the way a directed fuzzer is, runs first; the model is called when it stops reaching anything new. States the model reaches become sweep cells. (4, 10, 14)
- **The engine is the only oracle.** Each proposal climbs a ladder. First it reaches the change: covered changed lines in the tick, measured by V8 coverage. Then it changes the output: a step-hash difference between the parent build and the changed build under the same seed and inputs, located by the first-difference tool. Then it fails: a checker or invariant failure, a restore that does not match, or an outcome test. The model never states an expected outcome; a difference between builds is sorted against the dispatch's intent by the coordinator. (1, 7, 8, 11, 12)
- **Filters and mutants.** A proposal counts only after the checker admits it and it replays to the same hashes twice and across a restore. Mutants planted in the changed code measure the instrument, and a proposal that separates a mutant from the change is a catch. (1, 2, 13)
- **Measured against the cheap proposer, by budget.** Per GPU-hour and wall-clock: admission rate, changed lines reached, hash differences, mutants caught, unique findings. The report states plainly when the model did not beat the cheap proposer. (3, 5, 6)
- **Where it runs.** On the local GPU on demand, writing session records and T5 bundles; CI replays the records and never runs a model. (24 to 31)

## Not in this dispatch

No verdict from a model (2, 8). No expected outcome from a model (7). No model-drafted predicate (18). No role for characters in play thawed (36). No text from a player reaching the law (33, 34).
